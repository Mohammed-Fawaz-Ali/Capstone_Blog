Teaching a Phone to Spot Skin Patterns: What Our DermaLens Capstone Taught Us

Sixteen weeks, one EfficientNet-B4 model and a few humbling lessons about transfer learning, imbalanced data and building responsibly.

By the DermaLens team, SR University (School of CS&AI)Guided by Mr. Y. Srikanth3 min read

Open any skin-care forum and you will find the same question: “What is this patch on my skin?” Many skin conditions look alike, and specialists are not always within reach. For our capstone project, our team of five set out to build a small, honest answer: an app that takes a photo and shows the three most likely matches out of 22 conditions, as education and never as a diagnosis.

The idea in one picture

The app follows five simple steps: take a photo, resize it to 380 by 380 pixels, pass it through a neural network, rank the possible conditions by confidence, and let the user save the result. Everything about the image happens on the phone itself.

Why we did not train from scratch

Think of hiring a new colleague who already knows how to see edges, textures and shapes, and then teaching them dermatology. That is transfer learning. We started from EfficientNet-B4, a network pre-trained on millions of everyday images, and swapped its last layer for a new one with 22 outputs.

We trained in two stages. First we froze the pre-trained layers and trained only the new classifier for six epochs. Then we unfroze everything and fine-tuned for up to eighteen more at a smaller learning rate. The effect was dramatic: validation accuracy climbed from about 40 percent to about 77 percent. The single biggest gain in the whole project came from letting the pre-trained layers adapt.

The trap hidden in imbalanced data

Our dataset has 15,444 images, but some conditions appear about seven times more often than others. A model can look accurate simply by favouring the big classes, so we stopped trusting plain accuracy. We balanced the training batches, weighted the loss function, and chose our model by macro-F1, a score that treats every class equally.

On 1,546 images the model had never seen, it reached 76.65 percent accuracy and a macro-F1 of 0.739. Distinctive conditions such as vitiligo and warts scored well, while lupus, moles and drug eruptions stayed the hardest. We also trained and compared several other architectures, and EfficientNet-B4 gave the best macro-F1 among them.

When the heat-map surprised us

Numbers tell you how often a model is right, not why. We used Grad-CAM, a technique that paints a heat-map over the regions that influenced a prediction. In one example the model predicted a blistering condition with about 91 percent confidence, yet the strongest heat was in a corner of the photo, away from the lesion.

It was a single example, but it taught us that high confidence is not the same as understanding. That is exactly why showing the evidence matters.

From notebook to phone

We exported the trained model to the ONNX format and built DermaLens with Flutter, so inference runs on the device without sending the photo anywhere. Around the scanner we added a chatbot for general questions, a skin-care store and a nearby-clinic search. We were careful to be open about the trade-offs: chatbot messages and location searches do use external services, and some features, such as checkout, are demonstrations only. Every result screen reminds the user that this is educational screening, not a diagnosis.

What we would tell the next team

  1. Start from a strong pre-trained model, and fine-tune in stages.
  2. Choose your metric before your model, especially when classes are uneven.
  3. Keep the test set sealed until the very end.
  4. Look inside your model. A confident answer can still look at the wrong place.
  5. Write the disclaimer on day one, and mean it.

Our next steps are cross-validation, bringing Grad-CAM into the app, and testing across more skin tones before anyone considers real-world use.
